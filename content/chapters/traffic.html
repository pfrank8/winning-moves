Here is the map. Every car starts at S and wants to reach E. In between are two towns, A and B, joined to S and E by four roads. Two are wide highways: they take 45 minutes whether they carry one car or ten thousand. Two are narrow roads that clog: a narrow road carrying x cars takes x ÷ 100 minutes. 1,000 cars, 10 minutes; 4,000 cars, 40.

So a driver has two routes. The top route S→A→E takes the narrow road first and the highway second. The bottom route S→B→E does the opposite. Every morning 4,000 cars leave S. Each driver is a player, and each wants exactly one thing: the shortest trip.

Drag the slider to decide how many of the 4,000 take the top route; the rest take the bottom. Your own car is the red dot (choose your route with the buttons) and the blue dots are everybody else. Find the split where nobody wants to switch: no driver on either route could save even a second by taking the other one. That is a Nash equilibrium, the idea from Chapter 3, except that this game has 4,000 players instead of two. When you think you have it, press Check. Then press Let the drivers choose and watch the crowd find it without you: each round, some drivers on the slower route give up and switch, which is the best-response idea from Chapter 2 played by thousands of people at once.

The new road

Two thousand cars each way, 65 minutes for everybody. The mayor has an idea: build a bridge from A to B, so short and so wide that it takes 0 minutes. A brand-new road cannot possibly hurt anyone. Can it?

Open the bridge below. There is now a third route, S→A→B→E: narrow road, bridge, narrow road, no highway at all. With the old 2,000 and 2,000 split each narrow road carries 2,000 cars, so the new route takes 20 + 0 + 20 = 40 minutes. The drivers have settled with the bridge open. Would closing the bridge make anyone worse off?

Everybody ends up on the bridge route, and everybody takes 80 minutes. Both narrow roads now carry all 4,000 cars, 40 minutes each, and the 0-minute bridge in the middle saves nothing. Fifteen minutes worse than before the bridge existed.

The strange part: nobody wants to switch back. Leave the bridge route for the top route and you still share the S→A road with the other 3,999 cars: 40 minutes, then 45 on the highway, 85. The bottom route is 85 too. So 80 is the best any single driver can do, and the whole crowd is stuck there. Closing the bridge would send everyone back to 65. A road that hurts every driver who uses it, and that no driver can stop using: this is Braess's paradox, and it happens to real cities (see the story at the end).

Why? A driver on the bridge route uses both narrow roads and slows down every car on both of them. That driver feels the 40 minutes of clogging they suffer, but not the clogging they cause for 3,999 other people. Chapter 20 is about that kind of hidden cost.

The price of anarchy

How much does selfishness cost? Imagine a traffic planner who assigns every car to a route and cares only about the total time of all the trips added together (the smallest total is also the smallest average trip). Compare the selfish equilibrium with the planner's best plan, as a ratio:

price of anarchy = total time at the selfish equilibrium ÷ total time under the best plan

Game theorists really do call it the price of anarchy (say: AN-ar-kee, meaning "nobody in charge"). A price of 1 means selfish players do as well as any planner could. Bigger means worse.

With the bridge open, the equilibrium costs 4,000 × 80 = 320,000 car-minutes. The obvious plan ignores the bridge: 2,000 and 2,000, 65 minutes each, 260,000 car-minutes. But the planner can do slightly better. Send 1,750 cars on each old route and only 500 over the bridge. The narrow roads carry 2,250 cars each, so the old routes take 67.5 minutes, the bridge route 45, and the average is 64.6875 minutes: 258,750 car-minutes in total. The Math corner proves nothing beats it. Notice that this plan is nowhere near an equilibrium: 3,500 drivers sit through 67.5 minutes so that 500 can enjoy 45, and every one of the 3,500 would switch the moment they could. That is the whole problem. The best plan for the crowd is not a resting place for the people in it.

So the price of anarchy of this map is 320,000 ÷ 258,750 ≈ 1.24: selfish driving costs about 24 percent. Before the bridge existed the price was exactly 1, because the 2,000 and 2,000 split was already the best plan there was. The bridge is what opened the gap. Try it yourself: set the three flows by hand and see how close to 258,750 you can get.

The cleanest example of all has just two roads and is more than a hundred years old (Arthur Pigou, 1920). Road 1 always takes 1 hour. Road 2 takes x hours, where x is the fraction of the cars using it: empty, no time at all; with every car on it, an hour. Selfish drivers all pick road 2, which is never slower than road 1, so everyone takes 1 hour. The planner sends half the cars each way: half take 60 minutes, half take 30, and the average is 45. Price of anarchy: 60 ÷ 45 = 4/3.

In 2002 Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos proved something remarkable about that number. Take any network at all, with any number of roads, towns, and cars, as long as each road's travel time is a straight-line function of the cars on it (like x ÷ 100 + 45). Then the price of anarchy is never more than 4/3. Pigou's two roads are the worst case there is, and our bridge map, at 1.24, is not far behind.

Math corner

The equilibrium as an equation. Let x be the number of cars on the top route, so 4000 − x take the bottom. Nobody wants to switch when the two routes take the same time:

x/100 + 45 = 45 + (4000 − x)/100

Take 45 from both sides and multiply by 100: x = 4000 − x, so x = 2000, and both routes take 2000/100 + 45 = 65 minutes. There is no other solution, which is why the drivers end up in the same place wherever they start.

The bridge as inequalities. With all 4,000 cars on the bridge route the trip is 4000/100 + 0 + 4000/100 = 80. A driver who switched to the top route would find 4000/100 + 45 = 85 there, and 85 on the bottom route too. Since 80 ≤ 85 both ways, nobody switches. The general rule: every route in use is at least as fast as every route a driver could move to.

The planner's total. Write a plan as a cars on the top route, b on the bottom, and c over the bridge, with a + b + c = 4000. The S→A road carries a + c cars and the B→E road carries b + c, so the total minutes of all the trips is

(a + c)²/100 + 45a + 45b + (b + c)²/100

(each narrow road's cars times its minutes, plus 45 for every highway trip). The map is its own mirror image, top for bottom, so the best plan has a = b: if a lopsided plan were best, its mirror image would be exactly as good, and the plan halfway between them would beat both, because squares curve upward. Put c = x and a = b = (4000 − x)/2, so a + c = (4000 + x)/2, and the total becomes

(4000 + x)²/200 + 45(4000 − x) = x²/200 − 5x + 260,000 = (x − 500)²/200 + 258,750

Check the last step by multiplying out (x − 500)². A square is never negative, so the total is smallest at x = 500, where it is exactly 258,750: the 1,750 / 1,750 / 500 plan. At x = 0 it is 260,000, the "ignore the bridge" plan, just as it should be.

Why this is a Nash equilibrium. There are 4,000 players. Each one's strategy is a route and each one's payoff is minus their travel time. A split is a Nash equilibrium exactly when no single driver can lower their own time by changing route while the other 3,999 stay put. One car changes a narrow road by 1/100 of a minute, which is why "nobody wants to switch" comes out the same as "every route in use takes the same time". Traffic engineers call this a Wardrop equilibrium, after John Glen Wardrop, who wrote the rule down in 1952, two years after Nash.

Big idea

When thousands of players each choose what is best for themselves, the crowd settles into an equilibrium, and an equilibrium is not the same thing as the best plan. Adding an option can make the equilibrium worse for every single player. The price of anarchy measures the gap: how much worse the selfish equilibrium is than what a planner could arrange.

Try it on paper

1. Rush hour grows: 6,000 cars instead of 4,000, and no bridge. What is the equilibrium split, and how long does everyone take?

Answer

Same equation, new number: x/100 + 45 = 45 + (6000 − x)/100 gives x = 3000. Three thousand each way, and everyone takes 3000/100 + 45 = 75 minutes. Check it on the first board with "Cars in all" set to 6,000.

2. Now open the bridge with those 6,000 cars. Is everyone still on the bridge route at the equilibrium? If not, find the real one. (At an equilibrium, every route with cars on it takes the same time.)

Answer

No. With all 6,000 on the bridge route the narrow roads take 60 minutes each, 120 in all, but the top route would take 60 + 45 = 105, so drivers leave the bridge route. The true equilibrium uses all three routes: 1,500 on top, 1,500 on the bottom, 3,000 over the bridge. Each narrow road then carries 4,500 cars (45 minutes), so the top route is 45 + 45 = 90, the bottom is 90, and the bridge route is 45 + 0 + 45 = 90. Everyone takes 90 minutes, still 15 worse than the 75 they had without the bridge. The paradox does not need everyone on the new road, only enough of them.

3. Pigou's roads, but road 2 is worse at clogging: it takes 2x hours when a fraction x of the cars use it. Find the selfish equilibrium, the planner's best plan, and the price of anarchy.

Answer

Equilibrium: road 2 fills until it is as slow as road 1, so 2x = 1: half the cars on each road, and everyone takes 1 hour. Planner: the average is (1 − x) + 2x², which equals 2(x − 1/4)² + 7/8 (multiply it out to check), so the smallest average is 7/8 of an hour, 52.5 minutes, with a quarter of the cars on road 2. Price of anarchy: 60 ÷ 52.5 = 8/7, about 1.14. Under 4/3, as the theorem promises. The Pigou board has a 2x setting so you can watch it.

Challenge

Two choices. Either design your own map where adding a road makes every trip longer (new numbers, or a new shape), or explain why closing a road in a real city might help, and why it might not. Either way, name the ingredient that makes the paradox possible.

Answer

The ingredient is a road that clogs, used by drivers who pay for the delay they suffer but not for the delay they cause. The recipe: two routes, each with one clogging road and one steady road, with the clogging roads at opposite ends; then a shortcut joining the clogging roads lets a driver use both of them and skip the steady ones. Any numbers with that shape work: try 30 instead of 45, or narrow roads at x ÷ 50.

Closing a real road helps only if it is a road like the bridge; closing an ordinary one just crowds the others. The numbers matter too: on this very map the bridge helps when traffic is light (with 2,000 cars the bridge route takes 40 minutes against 55 without it) and only starts to hurt above 3,000 cars. Set "Cars in all" to 2,000 on the bridge board and watch. Real cities check with a model before they close anything. The paradox is not magic, it is arithmetic.

True story

Dietrich Braess, a German mathematician, published the paradox in 1968 with a map shaped just like this chapter's. On Earth Day in 1990, New York City closed 42nd Street, one of the busiest streets in Manhattan, and traffic nearby moved better, not worse; the New York Times headline that December was "What if They Closed 42d Street and Nobody Noticed?" In 2005 Seoul tore down the Cheonggyecheon elevated highway, which had carried about 168,000 cars a day through the middle of the city, and replaced it with a restored stream and a park. The predicted traffic disaster never came, and by most measures trips through the area got better. Christos Papadimitriou gave the "price of anarchy" its name in 2001, and Tim Roughgarden's 2005 book Selfish Routing and the Price of Anarchy made the name standard.
