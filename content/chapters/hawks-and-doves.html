Two animals arrive at the same worm at the same moment. The worm is worth V points to whoever eats it. Each animal has one of two habits. A Hawk fights for the worm. A Dove puffs itself up and displays for a while, but backs off the moment the other one actually attacks.

So there are three kinds of meeting:

  • Hawk meets Dove. The dove backs off. The hawk gets V, the dove gets 0.
  • Dove meets Dove. Both display, nobody attacks, and in the end they share: V/2 each.
  • Hawk meets Hawk. They fight. The winner eats the worm, worth V. The loser gets hurt, which costs C points. Before the fight starts each hawk has a half chance of winning, so on average a hawk in a fight gets (V − C)/2.

That is the whole game. It is called the Hawk-Dove game, and the grid below shows it with V = 10 and C = 20. Look at the top-left cell: Hawk meets Hawk is −5 each. A fight over a 10-point worm costs more than the worm is worth, so two hawks both come out worse than if they had never met. Fighting is expensive. Move the sliders and watch how the cost of fighting and the value of the prize change every cell.

What fraction of hawks?

Now picture a whole population of these animals. Every day each one meets a random other animal over a worm. Nobody decides to be a hawk or a dove. Each animal is born one or the other, and the animals that collect more points have more babies of their own kind. That last sentence is the whole engine. A habit that earns more points spreads. A habit that earns fewer points shrinks.

Say a fraction p of the population are hawks. (If p = 0.3, then 30 percent are hawks and 70 percent are doves.) When a hawk meets a random animal, the other animal is a hawk with probability p and a dove with probability 1 − p. So a hawk's average payoff is:

EH = p · (V − C)/2 + (1 − p) · V

A dove gets 0 against a hawk and V/2 against a dove, so:

ED = p · 0 + (1 − p) · V/2 = (1 − p) · V/2

The letter E stands for expected value: the average you get if you play many times. If EH > ED, hawks are doing better, so next year there are more hawks and p goes up. If EH < ED, p goes down. The population settles exactly where the two are equal. So set EH = ED and solve for p. Every step is written out, because this is the part worth being able to redo yourself.

  1. Start with the two averages set equal:
    p(V − C)/2 + (1 − p)V = (1 − p)V/2
  2. Subtract (1 − p)V/2 from both sides. The right side becomes 0. On the left, a whole (1 − p)V minus half of it leaves half of it:
    p(V − C)/2 + (1 − p)V/2 = 0
  3. Multiply everything by 2 to clear the halves:
    p(V − C) + (1 − p)V = 0
  4. Multiply out both brackets:
    pV − pC + V − pV = 0
  5. The pV and −pV cancel:
    V − pC = 0
  6. Add pC to both sides and divide by C:
    pC = V, so p = V/C

The population settles at a fraction of hawks equal to the prize divided by the cost:

p* = V / C

With V = 10 and C = 20, p* = 10/20 = 1/2: half hawks, half doves. Make the worm juicier (bigger V) and there are more hawks. Make injuries worse (bigger C) and there are fewer. And if C ≤ V, then V/C ≥ 1, which is not a fraction of anything. In that case a hawk earns at least as much as a dove no matter what p is, and hawks take over completely.

Let it evolve

Time to stop calculating and let the animals do it. Below are 300 animals, red hawks and blue doves. Each generation, every animal is paired with a random partner and the pair plays the worm game once. Then the next generation is born: each kind gets a share of the 300 new animals in proportion to the total points its members collected. Points can be negative, so the program adds C/2 to every animal's score first. That makes every score positive without changing which kind is doing better, and the Math corner says why. Nobody in the simulation knows what V/C is. Nobody solves an equation. They meet, eat, get hurt, and have babies.

Rock, paper, scissors, lizard

Hawks and doves settle down. Some games never do. On dry hillsides in California lives the side-blotched lizard, Uta stansburiana, and its males come in three throat colors. Orange males are big and aggressive and hold large territories with many females. Blue males hold a small territory and guard one mate closely. Yellow males have throats striped like a female's. They hold no territory at all; they sneak in and mate while nobody is looking.

Orange beats blue: orange males simply take over blue territories. Blue beats yellow: a male guarding one mate closely spots the sneaker. Yellow beats orange: an orange male with a huge territory cannot watch all of it, so the sneakers get through. That is exactly rock, paper, scissors, and it has the same consequence. Whenever one color becomes common, the color that beats it starts winning and spreads, and then gets beaten in turn by the third. The biologists Barry Sinervo and Curt Lively watched a real population for six years and saw the three colors take turns being the most common. They published it in Nature in 1996.

One more dose of reality: 1 newborn in 200 gets a random color, so no color ever vanishes completely. Watch the three lines chase each other around.

Math corner

The gap between hawk and dove. Subtract the two averages and simplify:

EH − ED = p(V − C)/2 + (1 − p)V/2 = (V − pC)/2

This is positive when pC < V, that is when p < V/C, and negative when p > V/C. Hawks gain when they are rarer than V/C and lose when they are more common than V/C. The population is pushed toward p* from both sides, which is why the simulation lands on the same line whether it starts at 5 percent or 95 percent.

Why adding C/2 is fair. The simulation's rule is pnext = p · FH / (p · FH + (1 − p) · FD), where FH = EH + C/2 and FD = ED + C/2 are the shifted averages. The smallest a hawk's average can be is (V − C)/2, so adding C/2 makes every score at least V/2, which is positive. Adding the same number to both averages does not change which one is bigger, so p goes up exactly when EH > ED, and stays put exactly when they are equal. The equilibrium does not move. Biologists call this update rule the replicator equation, and this whole subject evolutionary game theory.

Evolutionarily stable strategy. (say: ee-vuh-LOO-shun-air-uh-lee) A strategy is an ESS if, once nearly everyone uses it, any small group trying something else earns less and dies out. When C > V the ESS is the mix "be a hawk with probability V/C": a group of extra hawks pushes p above V/C and starts losing, and so does a group of extra doves in the other direction. When C ≤ V, Hawk is the ESS. A lone dove among hawks gets 0 while the hawks get (V − C)/2, which is at least 0, and whenever a hawk meets that dove it grabs the whole worm while a dove would only get half.

Back to Chapter 4. There you found a mixed strategy by choosing the odds that make the other player indifferent. Look at what we just solved: EH = ED says exactly that a hawk and a dove earn the same, so nobody has a reason to switch, and p = V/C is exactly the mixed Nash equilibrium (Chapter 3) of the one-shot Hawk-Dove game, in which each player plays Hawk with probability V/C. Same equation, same answer. The difference is who does the mixing. In Chapter 4 a player mixes on purpose. Here no animal mixes at all. Each one is a pure hawk or a pure dove, and the population as a whole is mixed at exactly the Nash odds. Evolution did the arithmetic. (The one-shot game also has two pure equilibria, Hawk against Dove and Dove against Hawk, but in a population where everybody looks alike nobody can tell who is supposed to be which. The Challenge fixes that.)

Big idea

A population that keeps playing the same game and copies whatever earns the most drifts to an equilibrium with nobody calculating anything. You find that equilibrium the same way you find a mixed strategy: set the two average payoffs equal and solve. For hawks and doves the answer is p* = V/C. The fraction of fighters is the prize divided by the cost of losing.

Try it on paper

1. V = 6 and C = 12. What fraction of the population ends up hawks?

Answer

p* = V/C = 6/12 = 1/2. Half hawks. Check it in the simulation: it lands on the same line as V = 10, C = 20, because only the ratio matters.

2. V = 8 and C = 6. What happens?

Answer

V/C = 8/6, which is more than 1, so there is no fraction where hawks and doves tie. Look at the gap: EH − ED = (V − pC)/2 = (8 − 6p)/2, and since p is at most 1, that is at least (8 − 6)/2 = 1. Hawks always earn more, so they take over completely. A fight over an 8-point worm that costs only 6 to lose is worth having.

3. V = 10, C = 20, and right now p = 1/4. Who is doing better, hawks or doves, and by how much?

Answer

EH = (1/4)(−5) + (3/4)(10) = −1.25 + 7.5 = 6.25. ED = (3/4)(5) = 3.75. Hawks are ahead by 2.5 points, so p is rising toward 1/2. The shortcut gives the same gap: (V − pC)/2 = (10 − 5)/2 = 2.5.

Challenge

Add a third kind of animal, Bourgeois (say: boor-ZHWAH): if it reached the worm first, it fights like a hawk; if it arrived second, it backs off like a dove. Suppose that at every meeting each animal is equally likely to have been first. (a) Show that two Bourgeois animals never fight and that each gets V/2 on average. (b) Take V = 10 and C = 20 and a population settled at half hawks, half doves. Work out what a single Bourgeois animal earns there. Careful: the answer is a tie, not a win. Then argue why Bourgeois invades anyway. (c) Once everyone is Bourgeois, show that neither a lone hawk nor a lone dove can invade.

Answer

(a) When two Bourgeois meet, one was first and one was second. The first fights, the second backs off, and there is no fight. Each animal is first half the time, so it gets V half the time and 0 the other half: V/2 on average, with no injuries ever.

(b) At p = 1/2, hawks and doves both earn ED = (1/2)(5) = 2.5. A Bourgeois animal fights half the time and backs off half the time, so against animals who cannot see who was first it behaves exactly like a coin flip between hawk and dove: half of 2.5 plus half of 2.5, which is 2.5. A tie. So at first Bourgeois does not spread. But look at what happens when two of them meet: they each get 5, while a hawk or a dove meeting a Bourgeois only gets 2.5 (a hawk gets half of −5 and half of 10; a dove gets half of 0 and half of 5). Every extra Bourgeois helps the other Bourgeois and does nothing for the hawks and doves. So once there are a few of them, they pull ahead, and the mix of hawks and doves is not stable against them.

(c) In a world of Bourgeois, a lone hawk meets an owner half the time (a fight, worth −5 on average) and an intruder half the time (a free worm, 10): average 2.5. A lone dove gets 0 against owners and shares 5 with intruders: average 2.5. The Bourgeois residents earn 5. Both invaders lose, so Bourgeois is an ESS whenever C > V. John Maynard Smith gave the strategy its name, and it is one reason animals so often respect "I was here first": a rule that says who backs off ends the fight before it starts.

True story

Biologists had long puzzled over why fights between animals of the same species so rarely end in death, even among animals with horns, fangs, and stings. In 1973 John Maynard Smith and George Price published "The Logic of Animal Conflict" in Nature, introduced the Hawk strategy and the idea of an evolutionarily stable strategy, and showed that a population does not need to be noble for the good of the species: a mix of fighters and displayers is simply stable, because once fighters are common, fighting stops paying. (Their gentle strategy was called Mouse in that paper; Dove came later.) Price was a strange and brilliant man, a chemist who taught himself evolution, wrote down an equation that describes natural selection in general, gave away nearly everything he owned to homeless people in London, and died in 1975. Maynard Smith went on to write the book that made evolutionary game theory a field, Evolution and the Theory of Games, in 1982. The rock-paper-scissors lizards were found in California by Barry Sinervo in the 1990s, and their three-colored game is now in the textbooks.
