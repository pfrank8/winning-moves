Part IV · Chance, crowds, and clever puzzles

Where to put the ice cream cart

A beach, a hundred customers, and two ice cream carts. Every customer walks to the nearer cart. Where should you park? Robo is asking the same question, and the answer is the strange part: both carts end up in the middle, right next to each other, while the customers at the ends get the longest walk. The same arithmetic runs elections.

Here is the setup. A beach runs from 0 to 100 meters, and 100 customers are spread along it, one every meter. You have an ice cream cart and so does Robo. Same ice cream, same price, so every customer walks to whichever cart is nearer. A customer standing exactly halfway between the carts counts half for each of you.

Your whole decision is where to park. Drag your cart, tap the sand, or use the slider. The dots turn red when they are yours and blue when they are Robo's. Then press Robo moves. Robo plays a best response, the idea from Chapter 3: given where you are, it picks the one spot on the whole beach that wins it the most customers.

Try to find a parking spot that Robo's best reply cannot beat. There is exactly one.

The beach

Every customer walks to the nearest cart. A halfway customer counts half for each.

Why the middle

Say you park at 30 and Robo parks at 70. The halfway point is 50, so everyone from 0 to 50 walks to you and everyone from 50 to 100 walks to Robo: 50 customers each, and nobody walks more than 30 meters.

Now Robo slides to 60. The halfway point moves to 45, so Robo gets everyone from 45 to 100, which is 55 customers, and you are down to 45. Robo did not win them by being nicer or cheaper. Every meter it moved toward you took half a meter of beach from you.

You can do the same back. Slide to 59, just to Robo's left, and you take everyone from 0 to 59.5: that is 59.5 customers. Robo answers by hopping to 58. Each hop lands just past the other cart, on the side with the bigger crowd, and each hop moves the pair closer to the middle. The hopping stops only when there is no bigger side left, and that happens at exactly one place: both carts at 50. Press Take turns on the beach above and watch it happen from wherever you parked.

Both at 50 is a Nash equilibrium (Chapter 3): given where the other cart is, neither can gain a single customer by moving. Now count the cost to the customers. With both carts at 50, a customer near the end of the beach walks almost 50 meters. With the carts at 25 and 75, nobody walks more than 25, the total walking is cut in half, and each cart still gets 50. But 25 and 75 does not last, because from there each cart can grab more by sliding inward. Chapter 3 said it and this beach says it again: equilibrium is not the same as good.

The uneven beach

Real crowds are not spread evenly. Suppose the good sand is at the right end and the crowd piles up there. Where do the carts end up now?

Not at 50. The middle of the beach stops mattering; what matters is the middle of the crowd. Call the spot with exactly half the customers on each side the median (say: MEE-dee-un). Park at the median customer and, whichever side Robo takes, it can win at most half. Park anywhere else and Robo steps between you and the median and wins more than half. So both carts slide to the median customer, and there they stop.

Test it. Before you press Take turns, work out where the median is and park your cart there. Hint counts the customers on each side of you. Call it within 2 meters and that is a star.

Where the crowd is

Same rules. The crowd bunches toward the right end.

Three carts

Add a third cart, another Robo in purple, and something breaks. With two carts there was a resting place. With three there is none.

Here is why. Whatever the arrangement, one cart is in the middle, and it only gets the customers between the two halfway points on either side of it, a thin slice. The outside carts keep everyone beyond them. So the middle cart hops outside, just past one of the others, and grabs a big slice. But now a different cart is in the middle, and it hops. The outside carts creep inward to squeeze the middle one, the middle one jumps out, and the squeeze starts over. Press Take turns and watch the count of moves climb. It never settles.

Chapter 3 said every game has a Nash equilibrium. Read the fine print: the theorem only promises one if the players are allowed to mix, choosing at random with chosen odds (Chapter 4). The three-cart game does have a mixed equilibrium. In the version where a cart can park at any point on the beach, one such equilibrium has each cart pick a random spot between 25 and 75, every spot equally likely. No ice cream seller has ever parked by rolling dice. An equilibrium can exist on paper and mean nothing on the sand.

Three carts, no rest

Turn order: Robo, Robo 2, you. Each cart moves only when it can gain.

Candidates on a line

Now change the labels and nothing else. The beach becomes a line of opinions, from left to right. The customers become voters, each standing at the opinion they hold. The two carts become two candidates, each choosing what to stand for, and every voter votes for the candidate whose position is closer to their own.

You already know what happens, because the math has not changed. Both candidates move to the median voter, the voter with half the electorate on each side. If the voters lean right, both candidates lean right. Stand anywhere else and the other candidate steps between you and the median and wins. This is the median voter theorem, worked out by Duncan Black in 1948 and made famous by Anthony Downs in 1957, and it is one reason two candidates often sound so alike by election day. Chapter 14 counted votes; this is about where candidates stand.

The model is too simple in ways worth naming. Real elections have many issues at once, not one line, and with two or more lines the median trick stops working. Candidates cannot slide freely: people remember what they said last year. Some voters stay home when both candidates drift away from them. And with three candidates you get the three-cart mess. Even so, the pull toward the median is real.

The voters

Same board, new labels. Each voter picks the closer candidate.
Math corner

A count is a length. With one customer per meter, counting customers is the same as measuring beach. Suppose you are at x and Robo is at y, with x < y. The halfway point is (x + y)/2, and you get everyone to the left of it:

your customers = (x + y) / 2
Robo's customers = 100 − (x + y) / 2

Check: x = 30, y = 70 gives 50; x = 30, y = 60 gives 45. Now read the formula as instructions. Your count goes up when x goes up, so you want to move right, toward Robo. Robo's count goes up when y goes down, so Robo wants to move left, toward you. On this beach, "best response" means move toward the other cart, until you cannot get past. (Pass, and the two lines swap.)

The median. To find the median of a list, sort it and take the middle number. Five customers at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 90 have median 3: two below, two above. With an even count there are two middle numbers, and the median is halfway between them. On the even beach the 50th customer stands at 49.5 and the 51st at 50.5, so the median is 50.

Why the median and not the mean. The mean, the ordinary average, of 1, 2, 3, 4 and 90 is 100/5 = 20. One far-away customer dragged it to a spot with four customers on one side and one on the other, and a cart parked at 20 is easy to beat. The median does not care how far away the far customers are, only how many there are, which is all a cart cares about too.

Big idea

When two sellers compete for customers spread along a line, each one's best move is to slide toward the other, so both end up together at the median customer, even though the customers would be better served if the sellers spread out. With three sellers there is no resting place at all. Swap "sellers" for "candidates" and "customers" for "voters" and the same arithmetic explains elections.

Try it on paper

1. Carts at 20 and 80 on the even beach. Who gets how many?

Answer

The halfway point is (20 + 80)/2 = 50, so 50 each. The farthest customer, near the middle of the beach, walks about 30 meters. Not an equilibrium.

2. You are parked at 40. Where should Robo park to get the most customers, and how many does it get?

Answer

Just to your right, on the big side. On the meter marks Robo parks at 41, the halfway point is 40.5, and Robo takes everyone from 40.5 to 100: 59.5 customers. If Robo could park a hair to your right, it would take everything past 40, which is 60.

3. Five customers stand at 10, 20, 30, 90 and 95. Where is the median, and where is the mean?

Answer

The median is 30, the middle of the sorted list. The mean is (10 + 20 + 30 + 90 + 95)/5 = 245/5 = 49. Two carts on this beach end up at 30, not 49.

Challenge

The beach is the shore of a round lake instead of a straight line: 100 meters around, customers spread evenly, and no ends. Two carts. Where do they end up? Guess first. Many people say "opposite each other". Then pick any two spots for the carts and count who gets what.

Answer

Trick question: they end up wherever they started. On a circle, each cart's territory reaches halfway to the other cart in both directions. If the short way round from you to Robo is d meters, the long way is 100 − d, and you get half of each: d/2 + (100 − d)/2 = 50. That is 50 whatever d is, so no move ever gains a customer, and every arrangement is an equilibrium, including the two carts side by side. Opposite each other is what the customers would choose, since then nobody walks more than 25 meters, but nothing pushes the carts there.

Bonus: three carts on the lake, evenly spaced, is an equilibrium. Each gets a third. Moving within its own stretch changes nothing, squeezing in between the other two is worth a sixth, and parking on top of one of them is worth a quarter. The trouble on the straight beach came from its two ends.

True story

Harold Hotelling, an American statistician, published the beach in 1929 in a paper called "Stability in Competition", and the picture is still called Hotelling's beach. He was thinking about shops on a main street, and about why competitors end up next door to each other. Duncan Black, a Scottish economist, turned the same line into voters and candidates in 1948, and Anthony Downs made it the standard model of elections in his 1957 book An Economic Theory of Democracy. Hotelling also let his sellers set prices, and there his argument had a hole that took fifty years to find: in 1979 three economists showed that once prices can change, the sellers may want to move apart. The plain version, with fixed prices, is why you see two gas stations on the same corner, why coffee shops cluster, and why a chapter about ice cream ended up being about elections.
