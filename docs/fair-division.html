Part III · Playing with people

I cut, you choose

You cut the cake and Robo picks a piece. It is the oldest fair trick there is, and it works even when the two of you want different things. This chapter says exactly what fair means, shows procedures that guarantee it for two people and for three, and finds the spot where fairness splits into two ideas that are not the same.

Two people, one cake, nobody to referee. The rule every kid works out eventually: you cut the cake into two pieces, and Robo picks whichever piece it wants. You get the other one.

Play it. Slide the knife, press Cut, and Robo takes the piece it likes better. Where should you cut?

Cut and choose

You cut. Robo picks the piece it likes better. You get the other one.
Robo always takes the piece it values more. If the pieces tie, Robo takes the left one.

Cut at 40 and Robo takes the 60. Robo always takes the bigger piece, so the best you can do is make the two pieces equal. Cut at 50 and you are guaranteed exactly half. That is the whole idea of cut and choose: the cutter cuts so that it does not matter which piece the chooser takes.

Half by size is not half by value

Now the cake is half chocolate (left) and half vanilla (right), and you and Robo disagree about what it is worth. To you chocolate is worth three times vanilla, so the chocolate half holds 75% of the value and the vanilla half 25%. Robo likes them equally. Switch the board to "Chocolate and vanilla" and cut at 50: same size, but to you the left is worth 75% and the right 25%. Robo sees a tie, takes the left, and you are left with a quarter.

The safe cut is the same rule said more carefully: cut so that the two pieces are worth the same to you. The chocolate half carries 75 points of value over 50 units of length, 1.5 points per unit, so you reach 50 points at 50 ÷ 1.5 = 33.3 units. Cut there (the slider stops at 33 or 34) and both pieces are worth half to you, whichever one Robo takes.

Now watch Robo. To Robo the left piece is a third and the right is two thirds, so Robo takes the right. You get half by your taste and Robo gets two thirds by its taste: 116.7% of one cake between you, and nobody cheated. When people want different things, a good split can leave both of them with more than half.

Can you push it further? As long as you cut left of the middle, Robo prefers the right, so everything up to your cut is yours: cut at 40 and you get 60%, at 49 you get 73.5%, and at 50 the tie drops you back to 25%. The greedy cut is a bet that you know Robo's tastes exactly. The safe cut needs no information about Robo at all, and that is the point: it works against a chooser you know nothing about.

Three people and a moving knife

Cut and choose is a two-person trick. With three people, who cuts? If you cut three equal pieces and let the robots pick, Robo and Robo 2 might both want the same one.

In 1961 Lester Dubins and Edwin Spanier fixed this with one moving knife. A referee slides a knife slowly across the cake from left to right. The moment the piece left of the knife is worth exactly one third to you, you shout Stop. Whoever shouts first takes that piece and leaves; the other two split the rest with cut and choose.

This cake has three flavors, and you, Robo, and Robo 2 each weigh them differently; you can see only your own weights. The robots shout at exactly their thirds. Start the knife and shout when the readout says a third. Not before.

Why does everyone get at least a third? Say Robo shouts first. You had not shouted yet, so to you that piece was worth less than a third, the rest is worth more than two thirds, and cut and choose gives you at least half of that: a third. A division where every one of n people thinks they got at least 1/n is called proportional (say: pro-POR-shun-ul). Shouting early is the only way to lose.

You got a third. You also think Robo's piece is worth half the cake, and you would trade in a heartbeat. Proportional says "I got my share." It does not say "I would not rather have yours." A division where nobody would swap is called envy-free. Every envy-free division is proportional (the Math corner says why in one sentence); this table shows the reverse is false. For two people the two ideas coincide. With three they come apart, and that is where fair division gets hard.

A recipe with no envy in it

Is there a procedure that guarantees an envy-free split for three? Yes. John Selfridge found it in 1960 and John Conway found it again in 1993; neither published it, so it travelled by word of mouth. It never needs more than five cuts.

  1. You cut the cake into three pieces that are equal in your eyes.
  2. Robo looks at the pieces. If one is the biggest to Robo, Robo trims it until it ties with Robo's second biggest and sets the trimmings aside. If two already tie, Robo trims nothing.
  3. Robo 2 takes whichever piece it likes best.
  4. Robo takes next. If the trimmed piece is still on the table, Robo must take it.
  5. You take the last piece.
  6. If nothing was trimmed, done. Otherwise, whichever of Robo and Robo 2 took the trimmed piece is the taker; the other of those two cuts the trimmings into three parts equal in its eyes.
  7. The taker picks first from those parts, then you, then the one who cut them.

Why is nobody envious? You cut equal pieces, Robo 2 chose first, and Robo made its two favorites tie, so each of you is happy with round one. The trimmings round repeats the trick, and the one worry, that you might envy the taker's extra helping, disappears because the trimmed piece plus all the trimmings is one of your original equal pieces.

For four or more people the question stayed open for decades. Steven Brams and Alan Taylor found a procedure in 1995 with no limit on the number of cuts. Only in 2016 did Haris Aziz and Simon Mackenzie find one with a limit, and it is enormous: for n people, a tower of exponents six n's tall. Nobody will run it at a birthday party, but before 2016 nobody knew it could exist.

Math corner

Writing fairness down. Number the players 1 to n. Player i has a value function vi that turns any piece of cake into a number, with the whole cake worth 1, and values add when you glue pieces together. Each definition of fair is one line:

Proportional: vi(my piece) ≥ 1/n, for every player i
Envy-free: vi(my piece) ≥ vi(any other piece), for every player i

Envy-free implies proportional, in one sentence. If my piece is at least as good as every one of the n pieces by my measure, and the n pieces add up to 1, then mine is at least 1/n, because if it were smaller, all n pieces would be smaller than 1/n and could not add up to 1.

Two people. With n = 2 the definitions collapse into one: if v(mine) ≥ 1/2 then v(yours) = 1 − v(mine) ≤ 1/2. So cut and choose is envy-free: the cutter values both pieces at 1/2 and does not care which one goes; the chooser took the piece it values more. Nobody wants to swap.

Big idea

"Fair" is not a feeling. It is a definition, and there are two. Proportional: everyone thinks they got at least 1/n. Envy-free: nobody would trade. A good procedure makes fairness a guaranteed result of following the rules, without knowing what anyone else wants. Two people, one cut. Three, five cuts. Many: more cuts than atoms in the universe, and still nobody can complain.

Try it on paper

1. A cake's left half is worth 70 to you and its right half 30. Where do you cut so that the two pieces are worth the same to you?

Answer

You need 50 of the 70 points on the left: 5/7 of the left half, which is 5/7 × 1/2 = 5/14 of the whole cake, about 35.7% from the left edge. Check: 70 × 5/7 = 50.

2. Four people share a cake with a proportional procedure. What fraction is each person guaranteed? Can someone get more?

Answer

At least 1/4. And yes: when tastes differ, several people can all beat a quarter by their own measure, as you and Robo shared 116.7% of one cake. The guarantee is a floor, not a ceiling.

3. Two people use cut and choose. Can the result fail to be envy-free?

Answer

Only if the cutter cuts badly. The chooser took the piece it liked better, so it never envies, and a cutter who made the pieces equal in its own eyes does not care which one went. A cutter who cuts unequally may watch the chooser walk off with the piece it wanted. Cut at 50 on the chocolate and vanilla board to feel it.

Challenge

Now divide something nobody wants: a garden of weeds to pull, where the rows are different amounts of work to different people. Fair now means nobody thinks they got more than their share. What changes in cut and choose? And the hard one: write the moving-knife rule for three people so that everyone ends with at most a third of the work by their own measure. Be careful about who takes the piece.

Answer

Cut and choose for chores. The cutter still splits the garden into two halves that are equal work in the cutter's eyes. The chooser takes the piece that is less work by the chooser's measure. Both end up with at most half.

The moving knife for chores. Each player shouts when the left piece is exactly a third of the work to them, but the knife keeps moving until the last player shouts, and that player takes the piece. The other two shouted earlier, so to them the piece is at least a third of the work and the rest is at most two thirds; chores cut and choose gives each of them at most half of that, which is at most a third. If the first shouter took the piece, as with cake, the others would think the rest was more than two thirds, and one of them could end up with more than a third. For cake the first shout wins; for chores the last one does.

True story

In 1944, in occupied Poland, Hugo Steinhaus was in hiding under a false name, and he spent some of that time thinking about how to divide a cake. He posed the problem, solved it for three people, and his colleagues Stefan Banach and Bronisław Knaster found the "last diminisher" procedure, the first proportional method for any number of people; Steinhaus published it all in 1948. Dubins and Spanier turned last diminisher into the moving knife in 1961, Selfridge and Conway found the three-person envy-free recipe in 1960 and 1993, Brams and Taylor's unlimited procedure came in 1995, and Aziz and Mackenzie's bounded one in 2016. Eighty years on, nobody knows how many cuts are really needed.
