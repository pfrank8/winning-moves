Part IV · Chance, crowds, and clever puzzles

The road that made everything slower

Every morning 4,000 cars drive from S to E, and every driver picks whatever route looks fastest. The traffic settles into an equilibrium, exactly like the games in Chapter 3, only with 4,000 players. Then the city opens a brand-new bridge, and every trip gets 15 minutes longer. Nobody is lying and nobody is being silly. By the end of this chapter you will be able to say exactly why, and to measure what selfishness costs.

Here is the map. Every car starts at S and wants to reach E. In between are two towns, A and B, joined to S and E by four roads. Two are wide highways: they take 45 minutes whether they carry one car or ten thousand. Two are narrow roads that clog: with x cars on it, a narrow road takes x ÷ 100 minutes. 1,000 cars, 10 minutes; 4,000 cars, 40.

So a driver has two routes. The top route S→A→E takes the narrow road first and the highway second. The bottom route S→B→E does the opposite. Every morning 4,000 cars leave S. Each driver is a player, and each wants one thing: the shortest trip.

Drag the slider to decide how many of the 4,000 take the top route; the rest take the bottom. Your car is the red dot (pick your route with the buttons); the blue dots are everybody else. Find the split where nobody wants to switch: no driver could save even a second by taking the other route. That is a Nash equilibrium, from Chapter 3, with 4,000 players instead of two. Press Check when you think you have it. Then press Let the drivers choose and watch the crowd find it without you: each round, some drivers on the slower route switch, the best-response idea from Chapter 2 played by thousands at once.

The new road

Two thousand cars each way, 65 minutes for everybody. The mayor has an idea: a bridge from A to B, so short and so wide that it takes 0 minutes. A new road cannot possibly hurt anyone. Can it?

Open the bridge below. There is now a third route, S→A→B→E: narrow road, bridge, narrow road, no highway at all. With 2,000 cars on each narrow road the new route takes 20 + 0 + 20 = 40 minutes. Every driver sees that 40 and thinks: I am wasting 25 minutes on a highway. Press Let the drivers choose.

The drivers have settled with the bridge open. Would closing the bridge make anyone worse off?

Everybody ends up on the bridge route, and everybody takes 80 minutes. Both narrow roads now carry all 4,000 cars, 40 minutes each, and the 0-minute bridge saves nothing. Fifteen minutes worse than before the bridge existed.

The strange part: nobody wants to switch back. Leave the bridge route for the top route and you still share the S→A road with 3,999 cars: 40 minutes, then 45 on the highway, 85. The bottom route is 85 too. So 80 is the best any single driver can do, and the whole crowd is stuck there. Closing the bridge would send everyone back to 65. A road that hurts every driver who uses it, and that no driver can stop using: this is Braess's paradox, and it happens to real cities.

Why? A bridge-route driver uses both narrow roads and slows down every car on both. They feel the clogging they suffer, not the clogging they cause for 3,999 others. Chapter 20 is about hidden costs like that.

The price of anarchy

How much does selfishness cost? Imagine a traffic planner who assigns every car to a route and wants the smallest total of all the trip times (which is also the smallest average). Compare the selfish equilibrium with the planner's best plan, as a ratio:

price of anarchy =
selfish total ÷ best-plan total

The selfish total is the total time at the equilibrium; the best-plan total is the smallest total a planner can arrange. Game theorists really do call the ratio the price of anarchy (say: AN-ar-kee, "nobody in charge"). A price of 1 means selfish players do as well as any planner could. Bigger means worse.

With the bridge open, the equilibrium costs 4,000 × 80 = 320,000 car-minutes. The obvious plan ignores the bridge: 2,000 and 2,000, 65 minutes each, 260,000 car-minutes. But the planner can do slightly better: 1,750 cars on each old route and 500 over the bridge. The narrow roads carry 2,250 cars each, so the old routes take 67.5 minutes, the bridge route 45, and the average is 64.6875 minutes, 258,750 car-minutes in total. The Math corner proves nothing beats it. Notice that this plan is no equilibrium: 3,500 drivers sit through 67.5 minutes so that 500 can enjoy 45, and every one of them would switch the moment they could. The best plan for the crowd is not a resting place for the people in it.

So the price of anarchy here is 320,000 ÷ 258,750 ≈ 1.24: selfish driving costs about 24 percent. Before the bridge the price was exactly 1, because 2,000 and 2,000 was already the best plan. The bridge opened the gap.

The cleanest example has just two roads and is more than a hundred years old (Arthur Pigou, 1920). Road 1 always takes 1 hour. Road 2 takes x hours, where x is the fraction of the cars using it: empty, no time at all; every car, an hour. Selfish drivers all pick road 2, which is never slower than road 1, so everyone takes 1 hour. The planner sends half each way: half take 60 minutes, half take 30, average 45. Price of anarchy: 60 ÷ 45 = 4/3.

In 2002 Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos proved that 4/3 is as bad as it gets: on any network at all, as long as each road's travel time is a straight-line function of the cars on it (like x ÷ 100 + 45), the price of anarchy is never more than 4/3. Pigou's roads are the worst case; our bridge map, at 1.24, is not far behind.

Math corner

The equilibrium as an equation. Let x be the number of cars on the top route, so 4000 − x take the bottom. Nobody wants to switch when the routes take the same time:

x/100 + 45 = 45 + (4000 − x)/100

Take 45 from both sides and multiply by 100: x = 4000 − x, so x = 2000, and both routes take 65 minutes. There is no other solution.

The bridge as inequalities. With all 4,000 on the bridge route the trip is 4000/100 + 0 + 4000/100 = 80. A driver who switched to the top route would find 4000/100 + 45 = 85, and 85 on the bottom too. Since 80 ≤ 85 both ways, nobody switches. The rule: every route in use is at least as fast as every route a driver could move to.

The planner's total. Write a plan as a cars on top, b on the bottom, c over the bridge, with a + b + c = 4000. The S→A road carries a + c cars and B→E carries b + c, so the total minutes of all the trips is

(a + c)²/100 + (b + c)²/100
+ 45a + 45b

(each narrow road's cars times its minutes, plus 45 per highway trip). The map is its own mirror image, so the best plan has a = b (a lopsided plan and its mirror image tie, and the plan halfway between beats both, because squares curve upward). Put c = x and a = b = (4000 − x)/2, so a + c = (4000 + x)/2, and the total becomes

(4000 + x)²/200 + 45(4000 − x)
= x²/200 − 5x + 260,000
= (x − 500)²/200 + 258,750

Check the last step by multiplying out (x − 500)². A square is never negative, so the total is smallest at x = 500, where it is exactly 258,750: the 1,750 / 1,750 / 500 plan.

Why this is a Nash equilibrium. There are 4,000 players; each one's strategy is a route and each one's payoff is minus their trip time. A split is a Nash equilibrium exactly when no single driver can lower their own time by changing route while the other 3,999 stay put. One car changes a narrow road by 1/100 of a minute, so that test comes out the same as "every route in use takes the same time". Traffic engineers call it a Wardrop equilibrium, after John Glen Wardrop, who wrote it down in 1952, two years after Nash.

Big idea

When thousands of players each choose what is best for themselves, the crowd settles into an equilibrium, and an equilibrium is not the same thing as the best plan. Adding an option can make the equilibrium worse for every single player. The price of anarchy measures the gap: how much worse the selfish equilibrium is than what a planner could arrange.

Try it on paper

1. Rush hour grows: 6,000 cars instead of 4,000, and no bridge. What is the equilibrium split, and how long does everyone take?

Answer

Same equation, new number: x/100 + 45 = 45 + (6000 − x)/100 gives x = 3000. Three thousand each way, 3000/100 + 45 = 75 minutes for everyone.

2. Now open the bridge with those 6,000 cars. Is everyone still on the bridge route at the equilibrium? If not, find the real one. (At an equilibrium, every route with cars on it takes the same time.)

Answer

No. With all 6,000 on the bridge route the trip is 60 + 60 = 120 minutes, but the top route would take 60 + 45 = 105, so drivers leave. The true equilibrium uses all three routes: 1,500 on top, 1,500 on the bottom, 3,000 over the bridge. Each narrow road then carries 4,500 cars, 45 minutes, so every route takes 90: still 15 worse than the 75 without the bridge. The paradox does not need everyone on the new road, only enough of them.

3. Pigou's roads, but road 2 is worse at clogging: it takes 2x hours when a fraction x of the cars use it. Find the selfish equilibrium, the planner's best plan, and the price of anarchy.

Answer

Equilibrium: road 2 fills until it is as slow as road 1, so 2x = 1: half the cars on each road, and everyone takes 1 hour. Planner: the average is (1 − x) + 2x² = 2(x − 1/4)² + 7/8 (multiply it out to check), smallest at x = 1/4: 7/8 of an hour, 52.5 minutes. Price of anarchy: 60 ÷ 52.5 = 8/7, about 1.14, under 4/3 as the theorem promises.

Challenge

Two choices. Design your own map where adding a road makes every trip longer (new numbers or a new shape), or explain why closing a road in a real city might help, and why it might not. Either way, name the ingredient that makes the paradox possible.

Answer

The ingredient is a road that clogs, used by drivers who pay for the delay they suffer but not the delay they cause. The recipe: two routes, each with one clogging road and one steady road, clogging roads at opposite ends; a shortcut joining the clogging roads then lets a driver use both and skip the steady ones. Any numbers of that shape work.

Closing a real road helps only if it is a road like the bridge; closing an ordinary one just crowds the others. And the numbers matter: on this very map the bridge helps when traffic is light (with 2,000 cars, 40 minutes against 55) and only hurts above 3,000 cars. Try it on the bridge board. Real cities check with a model before closing anything.

True story

Dietrich Braess, a German mathematician, published the paradox in 1968 with a map shaped just like this chapter's. On Earth Day 1990, New York City closed 42nd Street in Manhattan, and traffic nearby moved better, not worse; that December the New York Times asked "What if They Closed 42d Street and Nobody Noticed?" In 2005 Seoul tore down the Cheonggyecheon elevated highway, which had carried about 168,000 cars a day, and replaced it with a restored stream and a park. The predicted traffic disaster never came, and by most measures trips through the area got better. Christos Papadimitriou named the "price of anarchy" in 2001, and Tim Roughgarden's 2005 book Selfish Routing and the Price of Anarchy made the name standard.
