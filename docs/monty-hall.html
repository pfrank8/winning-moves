Part IV · Chance, crowds, and clever puzzles

The three doors

Three doors. One hides a bicycle, two hide goats. You pick a door, Robo opens a different door to show you a goat, and then asks: stay or switch? Nearly everyone says it makes no difference. Nearly everyone is wrong, and the reason is not the doors. It is what Robo knows.

Here is the game. Three closed doors. Behind one is a bicycle. Behind each of the other two is a goat. Robo is the host, and Robo knows exactly where the bicycle is. You pick a door. Robo then opens one of the other two doors, always one with a goat behind it, and asks: do you want to stay with your door, or switch to the one closed door that is left?

Most people say it cannot matter. Two doors left, one bicycle, so it is fifty-fifty either way. That is what almost everyone thinks the first time, including a great many people with mathematics degrees. Play by hand for a while and watch the two tallies, one for the rounds where you stayed and one for the rounds where you switched. Then, before you let Robo play a thousand rounds for you, make a prediction. The prediction is the star.

Why it is two thirds

Forget the switch for a moment and look at your first pick. Three doors, one bicycle, and you know nothing yet. Your first pick is right 1 time in 3 and wrong 2 times in 3, and nothing Robo does afterwards can reach back and change that.

Now follow each case to the end. If your first pick was right (1 time in 3), both other doors hide goats. Robo opens one, and switching takes you to the other goat. Switching loses. If your first pick was wrong (2 times in 3), the other two doors hide one bicycle and one goat. Robo is not allowed to open the bicycle, so Robo is forced to open the goat, and the only door left to switch to is the bicycle. Switching wins.

So switching wins exactly when your first pick was wrong, which is 2 times in 3. That is the whole proof. Here it is as a table, with you picking door 1. Picking door 2 or 3 works the same way with the doors renamed.

Your door was chosen blind. The other door survived an inspection: Robo looked at two doors, knowing where the bicycle was, and left that one shut. Two times in three, Robo had no choice.

If it still feels slippery, make the game bigger. A hundred doors, one bicycle. You pick one. Robo, who knows where the bicycle is, opens 98 goat doors and leaves exactly one other door closed. Your door, or the one Robo carefully stepped around?

It only works because the host knows

Here is the part most explanations skip, and it is the part that makes the puzzle honest. Everything above depends on Robo knowing where the bicycle is. Change one rule: Robo does not know, and opens one of the two other doors at random. Sometimes Robo opens the bicycle by accident. Call that round spoiled: you never got your choice, so it counts for neither staying nor switching. But suppose the random door shows a goat, exactly like before. Same doors, same goat, same question. Now switching wins only half the time.

Count the cases over 300 games. Your first pick is right in about 100 and wrong in about 200. When it is right, both other doors hide goats, so a guessing Robo shows a goat every time: 100 goat games. When it is wrong, a guessing Robo opens the bicycle half the time: about 100 spoiled games and 100 goat games. Throw out the spoiled games. Of the 200 that survive, 100 had a right first pick and 100 a wrong one, and switching wins only in the wrong ones: 100 out of 200, one half.

The knowing Robo never spoils a game, so all 200 wrong-first-pick games survive and switching wins 200 out of 300. The guessing Robo's accidents land entirely on the games where switching would have won. That is why the rate drops. Run it, then answer the question at the bottom of the board.

Conditional probability, gently

What you just did has a name. Whenever you ask "of the cases where B happened, what fraction are also cases where A happened," you are computing a conditional probability (say: kon-DISH-un-ul), written P(A given B). Mathematicians write the "given" as a vertical bar: P(A | B). It is a plain fraction: count the B-cases, count how many of them are also A-cases, and divide.

Here B is "Robo showed a goat" and A is "switching wins", which is the same as "your first pick was wrong". The tables count 300 imagined games with each host.

Knowing host: P(first pick wrong | goat shown) = 200 / 300 = 2/3. Guessing host: 100 / 200 = 1/2. Same goat on the screen, different fraction, because the goat was produced by a different process. Information is not only what you see. It is what you see together with how it came to be shown to you.

Updating what is likely after seeing evidence is called Bayes' rule (say: BAYZ), after Thomas Bayes, an English clergyman of the 1700s who worked out the idea. It is how a spam filter decides what a suspicious email probably is, and how a doctor works out what a positive test result really means. The count table is the whole idea; the formula people learn later is the count table written in letters.

Math corner

The two strategies. Switching wins exactly when the first pick was wrong. Staying wins exactly when it was right.

P(switch wins) = P(first pick wrong) = 2/3
P(stay wins) = P(first pick right) = 1/3

Expected value. From Chapter 4: multiply each outcome by its probability and add, counting a bicycle as 1 and a goat as 0.

EV(switch) = 2/3 × 1 + 1/3 × 0 = 2/3 of a bicycle per game
EV(stay) = 1/3 × 1 + 2/3 × 0 = 1/3 of a bicycle per game

More doors. With n doors and a knowing host who opens every other goat door, your first pick is wrong (n − 1) times in n, and each of those times the host must leave the bicycle closed.

P(switch wins) = (n − 1) / n     P(stay wins) = 1 / n

For n = 3 that is 2/3; for n = 100 it is 99/100. The hundred-door board takes any number from 3 to 100.

Conditional probability as counts.

P(A | B) = (cases with both A and B) ÷ (cases with B)

Knowing host: 200 ÷ 300 = 2/3. Guessing host: 100 ÷ 200 = 1/2. If you can count the cases, you never need anything fancier.

The Monty Fall problem. In 2008 the statistician Jeffrey Rosenthal named the guessing-host version the Monty Fall problem: the host slips, falls against a door, and it happens to swing open on a goat. Same doors, same goat, answer 1/2. The name makes the point that the answer depends on how the door came to be opened, not only on which door it was.

Big idea

What you know changes what is likely, and the safe way to work out how is to count cases. List every way the situation could have come about, keep only the ways that agree with what you saw, and see what fraction of those are the ones you care about. The same goat, shown by a host who knows and a host who guesses, means two different things.

Try it on paper

1. Four doors, one bicycle, three goats. You pick door 1. Robo, who knows, opens one goat door among the other three and offers you a switch to either of the two other closed doors. What is the chance of winning if you stay? If you switch to a particular one of the two?

Answer

Staying wins 1/4: your first pick was right 1 time in 4, and nothing afterwards changes it. Switching: your first pick is wrong 3/4 of the time, and then the bicycle is behind one of the two doors Robo left closed, each equally often. So a particular other door wins 3/4 × 1/2 = 3/8. Better than 1/4, but not 3/4, because Robo removed only one goat.

2. A sneaky host. Robo knows where the bicycle is and offers the switch only when your first pick was right; otherwise Robo just opens your door. Should you ever switch?

Answer

Never. If the switch is offered, your first pick was right, so switching loses every time. This is why the rules must say what the host always does. Change the host's rule and you change the answer.

3. In the hundred-door game, what is P(stay wins)? Bonus: what if Robo opens only 50 goat doors instead of 98, leaving 49 other doors closed?

Answer

1/100, however many doors Robo opens. Bonus: your first pick is wrong 99/100 of the time, and then the bicycle is equally likely behind each of the 49 other closed doors, so a particular one wins 99/100 × 1/49 = 99/4900, about 2%. Still twice as good as staying.

Challenge

Set up three cups and a coin and ask a friend to be the host. Before you start, they decide in secret whether to peek under the cups before each round or never peek, and keep to that choice all game. You must not watch the setup. Play 30 rounds, switching every time, and write down each round. Did your friend peek? How can you tell from the tally?

Answer

Look for spoiled rounds first. A host who peeks never turns over the coin by accident; a host who does not does it about 1 round in 3, so expect around 10 spoils in 30. Then look at switching wins among the surviving rounds: around 2/3 for a peeker, around 1/2 for a guesser. With only 30 rounds the win rate alone can fool you, but the spoiled count is hard to miss. One spoiled round proves your friend did not peek, and thirty rounds with no spoils from a guessing host happens less than 1 time in 100,000.

True story

The puzzle is named after Monty Hall, host of the American television game show Let's Make a Deal, which began in 1963. In 1990 a reader asked Marilyn vos Savant, who wrote a question-and-answer column in Parade magazine, whether to switch. She said yes: 2/3 for switching, 1/3 for staying. About ten thousand letters arrived, close to a thousand of them from people with PhDs, most insisting she was wrong. She was right. The mathematician Paul Erdős reportedly refused to accept the answer until a colleague showed him a computer simulation, which is what the first board on this page is. Monty Hall himself, asked about it in 1991, played the game with a reporter, got the same result, and pointed out that on the real show he was under no obligation to offer a switch at all.
