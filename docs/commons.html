Part IV · Chance, crowds, and clever puzzles

The pond that everyone owns

Four fishers share a pond. The fish grow back, but not fast enough for everyone to take as much as they want. This is the Cookie Game with four players and a future, and the pond is what crumbles. You will play it, watch it collapse, work out exactly how much it can give, and then build the rules that keep it alive.

Here is a pond. It belongs to nobody, which means it belongs to everybody: you and Robo 1, Robo 2, and Robo 3 all fish in it. It starts with 60 fish. Every season, each of the four of you decides how many fish to catch, from 0 to 10. Then the fish that are left breed, and a new season starts.

The regrowing is the whole point, so here is the rule exactly. If F fish are left after the catch, the pond adds

growth = 0.5 × F × (1 − F / 100)

new fish before the next season. Try it: 60 fish left gives 0.5 × 60 × 0.4 = 12 new fish. A full pond of 100 adds nothing, and so does an empty one, forever. Ask for more than the pond has and you split what is there in proportion to what you asked for.

Choose your catch with the slider and press Next season, or let it run. The robots come in flavors: Greedy always takes 10, Careful takes 3, Copycat takes whatever you took last season, and Mixed gives you one of each. Your goal: after 20 seasons the pond still has at least 40 fish, and you have caught at least 70. Careful robots make that possible. Taking 3 every season gets you 60; the last 10 takes some thinking.

Why it collapses

Start with the arithmetic. The pond never grows more than 12.5 fish in a season, and it only does that when exactly 50 are left after the catch (the Math corner shows why). So four fishers can take about 12 fish a season between them, forever. Three each works: the pond sits in the sixties and everyone catches 60 fish in 20 seasons. Four each is 16 a season, more than the pond can ever grow, so it shrinks a little, then a lot, and is empty by season 9. Ten each empties it in two seasons. After that, everyone catches nothing, forever.

Now the game theory. Put yourself in one fisher's boots. Whatever the other three do, taking one more fish this season gives you one more fish, right now. The cost is a slightly smaller pond next season, split four ways, arriving later. A whole fish for you now against about a quarter of a fish later. So taking more is a dominant strategy, exactly like Grab in the Cookie Game of Chapter 2, and every fisher can see the same arithmetic. Played for a single season, everyone taking 10 is the only Nash equilibrium (Chapter 3). With a known last season, thinking backwards (Chapter 5) unravels the 20-season game the way it unraveled the repeated Cookie Game in Chapter 11.

This is the tragedy of the commons. A commons is anything shared that nobody owns: a pond, a pasture, the air, the fish in the sea. The tragedy is that everyone's best move adds up to a disaster nobody wanted. Try the calculator: pick a catch for everyone and watch where the pond goes.

The public goods game

The same trap with coins instead of fish, stripped down so you can see the gears. Four players, 10 coins each. Each of you secretly puts some of your coins, 0 to 10, into a shared pot. The pot is then multiplied by 1.6 and split equally among all four players, whether they put anything in or not.

Two extremes first. If all four put in everything, the pot is 40, it becomes 64, and everyone walks away with 16 coins. If nobody puts in anything, everyone keeps 10. Now the gears. A coin you put in becomes 1.6 coins, split four ways, so 1.6 ÷ 4 = 0.4 of a coin comes back to you and 1.2 goes to the other three. Keeping it gives you the whole coin, so every coin you contribute costs you 0.6, no matter what anyone else does: contributing is dominated. The best thing for the group is for everyone to contribute everything, and the best thing for each player is to contribute nothing, and both are true at the same time. Grown-ups call this a public goods game, and someone who contributes nothing while collecting a share of the pot is a free rider.

Play 10 rounds. Everyone gets a fresh 10 coins each round, and what you end the round with piles up in your total. The robots come as Giver (puts in all 10), Free rider (puts in 0), Conditional (puts in whatever the other three averaged last round, starting at 5), or Mixed, one of each. Before you play, answer the question on the board.

Rules that work

So far this chapter is bad news. The good news: the players are allowed to change the game. Two fixes, both waiting in the boards above.

A quota with a fine. The fishers agree that nobody catches more than q fish a season, and anyone who does pays a fine. A fine is just a number in the payoff grid, and it has to be big enough. A Greedy robot facing a quota of 3 wants 7 extra fish. If the fine is 5, it cheats and comes out 2 ahead. If the fine is 8, it obeys. The robots do exactly this arithmetic: they cheat when the extra fish are worth more than the fine. Set three Greedy robots loose with the quota on, and find the smallest fine that keeps the pond alive.

Punishment. The coin game has no warden, so the players do it themselves: after each round, anyone can pay 1 coin to take 3 coins from someone. Punishing costs the punisher, so it is itself a small public good. With punishment on, a robot that gave at least the average punishes anyone who gave 3 or more coins less than the average, and a punished Free rider matches the others for three rounds before it tests you again. Watch the Conditional robots. Without punishment, a free rider drags the average down and they follow it. With punishment, the free rider is made to give, the average climbs, and they climb with it.

These are not toy fixes. Quotas, fines, and neighbors who will call you out are exactly the tools real communities use to keep a commons alive. All of them change the payoffs, so that the selfish move and the good-for-everyone move become the same move. You do not have to make people nicer. You have to make the game better.

Math corner

Logistic growth. The pond follows growth = r × F × (1 − F / K) with r = 0.5 and K = 100. r is how fast the fish breed when there is plenty of room, and K is the most fish the pond can hold (biologists call it the carrying capacity). The (1 − F / K) part is the fraction of the pond still empty. At F = 60 it is 0.4, so growth = 0.5 × 60 × 0.4 = 12. Small ponds grow slowly because there are few parents; nearly full ponds grow slowly because there is no room. The pond grows fastest when it is half full, and not at all when it is empty or completely full.

Exactly in the middle. Write the growth as 0.5 × F × (100 − F) / 100. The numbers F and 100 − F add up to 100, and the product of two numbers with a fixed sum is biggest when they are equal: (50 + d) × (50 − d) = 2500 − d², largest when d = 0. So growth peaks at F = 50, where it is 0.5 × 50 × 0.5 = 12.5, or r × K / 4 in symbols. Fishery scientists call this the maximum sustainable yield: the most you can take, season after season, without the pond shrinking.

The pot. With a multiplier m and n players, each coin you contribute returns m / n to you, here 1.6 / 4 = 0.4. Whenever m / n < 1, keeping the coin beats contributing it whatever the others do, so contributing is dominated. Whenever m > 1, the group gains from every coin. Both are true here at once, and that gap is the whole dilemma: it is Chapter 2's Grab beating Share, with four players and a pot.

Big idea

When many people share something that regrows, each person's best move is to take a little more, and the sum of everyone's best moves can destroy the thing. It is the Cookie Game with many players and a future. The way out is not to hope for nicer players but to change the payoffs: quotas, fines, and punishment that the players agree on and enforce themselves.

Try it on paper

1. Use the formula: how many fish does the pond add when 20, 50, and 80 are left after the catch?

Answer

0.5 × 20 × 0.8 = 8, 0.5 × 50 × 0.5 = 12.5, and 0.5 × 80 × 0.2 = 8. Twenty and eighty give the same growth: one has few parents, the other has little room.

2. Change the coin pot's multiplier from 1.6 to 4.4, still with 4 players. Is contributing still dominated?

Answer

No. Each coin now returns 4.4 / 4 = 1.1 to you, more than the 1 you would keep, so contributing everything is dominant and the dilemma is gone. The trap needs the multiplier to be smaller than the number of players.

3. Five fishers share the pond and each takes 4 fish a season. Can the pond keep that up?

Answer

No. That is 20 fish a season and the pond never grows more than 12.5. No pond size grows 20 fish back, so it shrinks every season wherever it starts, and eventually empties.

Challenge

Find the largest total catch per season that the four of you can keep up forever, and the pond size that supports it. First by experiment, with the calculator or with Careful robots, watching where the pond settles. Then by the formula.

Answer

The formula says 12.5 fish a season, with exactly 50 left after the catch (62.5 before it). With whole fish that means 12 a season, three each. Run the calculator at 3 and the line is still creeping upward at season 20: it is heading for 72 before the catch and 60 after, because 60 left grows by 0.5 × 60 × 0.4 = 12, exactly replacing the catch.

There is a second, dangerous answer. Forty left also grows by 12 (0.5 × 40 × 0.6 = 12). But 41 left grows by a little more than 12 and climbs, while 39 grows by a little less and slides. Sixty is a balance point the pond returns to; forty is a cliff edge. Keep a commons well above its cliff.

True story

The phrase comes from a 1968 essay in the journal Science called "The Tragedy of the Commons", by an ecologist with an excellent first name, Garrett Hardin. His example was a shared pasture where every herder adds one more animal, and his conclusion was gloomy: shared things get ruined unless somebody owns them or a government polices them. The political scientist Elinor Ostrom spent decades in the field checking whether that was true. She studied Swiss mountain meadows, Spanish irrigation canals, forests in Nepal, and lobster grounds in Maine, and found that communities often avoid the tragedy with rules they design, monitor, and enforce themselves. In 2009 she won the Nobel Prize in economics, the first woman to do so. The coin pot with punishment comes from experiments by the economists Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter, published in 2000 and 2002: people paid to punish free riders even when they would never meet them again, and contributions rose over the rounds instead of fading away.
